Three Lessons That Changed How I Set Goals

After 15 years of studying goal-setting, I've learned that most advice is wrong. Here are three distinctions that changed everything — just in time for 2025.

Vishen Lakhiani
December 29, 2024

It's that time of year again.

The gyms are about to get crowded. Vision boards are being assembled. Journals are being cracked open to pristine first pages. And millions of people are about to write down goals that — statistically speaking — most will abandon by February.

I don't say that to be cynical. I say it because I've lived it.

For years, I was one of those people. I'd write ambitious goals. I'd feel the rush of possibility. And then, somewhere around week three, reality would hit — and with it, the creeping sense that maybe I just wasn't disciplined enough, focused enough, good enough to make these things happen.

It took me 15 years of studying human transformation — and one life-changing program called Lifebook — to realize something:

The problem wasn't my discipline. It was my distinctions.

I was thinking about goals in ways that were fundamentally broken. Not because I was lazy. But because no one had ever taught me the difference between what sounds good and what actually works.

Today, as we stand on the edge of 2025, I want to share three distinctions that have completely transformed how I approach goals, growth, and the messy, beautiful process of becoming who we're meant to be.

These aren't productivity hacks. They're not "10x your results" clickbait. They're fundamental shifts in how you relate to your own ambition — and I believe they might change your life the way they changed mine.

• • •

Distinction #1: Baseline vs. Peak

Here's the first mistake almost everyone makes with goals:

They aim for their best day instead of their baseline.

Think about how most goals get set. You're feeling inspired — maybe it's New Year's Eve, maybe you just watched a documentary, maybe you're recovering from a rough week and you're finally "ready to change."

In that moment of peak motivation, you write down goals that require peak performance to achieve.

"I'll work out six days a week."
"I'll wake up at 5am every day."
"I'll meditate for an hour each morning."

And here's the problem: You're not going to be at peak motivation forever. Life will happen. Stress will come. Sleep will suffer. The honeymoon period will fade.

When that happens — and it always does — your ambitious peak-state goals become a source of shame rather than inspiration.

The Distinction

Baseline
Your sustainable default
vs
Peak
Temporary intensity

"A great life is built on baseline excellence, not constant peaks."

The solution is to design goals around your baseline — your sustainable default. Not the life you can maintain when everything is perfect, but the life you can maintain when things are hard.

Personal Example

Years ago, I set a goal to meditate for an hour every morning. On my best days, I did it. But on most days — the days with early meetings, sick kids, travel, or just plain exhaustion — I'd skip it entirely. Zero minutes. Because in my mind, if I couldn't do the full hour, why bother?

When I shifted to baseline thinking, I changed my goal to 6 minutes of meditation daily. That was my non-negotiable. Most days, I'd do more. But on my worst days, I'd still do six minutes. My baseline held — and over time, that consistency compounded into something far more powerful than sporadic hour-long sessions ever did.

Here's the counterintuitive truth: Lowering your minimum actually raises your average.

When you set a baseline you can maintain even on your worst days, you never break the chain. You never have to start over. And that continuity — that identity of being someone who always shows up — becomes the foundation for genuine transformation.

The Lesson

Don't ask: "What can I do on my best day?" Ask: "What can I maintain on my worst day?" That's your baseline. Protect it fiercely. Everything else is a bonus.

Distinction #2: Direction vs. Perfection

The second distinction addresses one of the biggest sources of paralysis I see in ambitious people:

The belief that you need to know exactly where you're going before you can start.

I can't tell you how many brilliant, capable people I've met who are stuck — not because they lack talent or resources, but because they're waiting for perfect clarity. They want to know the exact job, the exact partner, the exact city, the exact path before they take the first step.

Here's what I've learned: That clarity almost never comes from thinking. It comes from moving.

The Distinction

Direction
Gives calm
vs
Perfection
Creates paralysis

"You don't need certainty. You need a direction your body agrees with."

You don't need to see the whole staircase. You just need to know: Am I generally going up or down? Am I moving toward more aliveness, more contribution, more authenticity — or away from it?

Direction is enough.

Think of it like driving at night. Your headlights only illuminate a few hundred feet ahead. You can't see your final destination. But you can see enough to keep moving — and as you move, more of the road reveals itself.

Goals work the same way.

What This Looks Like in Practice

Instead of: "I need to find my perfect career path."
Try: "I want to move toward work that feels more creative and meaningful."

Instead of: "I need to find my soulmate."
Try: "I want to move toward deeper connection and emotional availability."

Instead of: "I need to know exactly how much money I want to make."
Try: "I want to move toward financial spaciousness and reduced money stress."

Notice how direction-based goals create calm, while perfection-based goals create pressure.

When you're aiming for direction, every step in roughly the right direction counts. You're not failing if you haven't "arrived" — because there's no fixed arrival point. There's just continued movement toward more of what matters.

This is especially important for those of us who tend toward perfectionism. We've been trained to believe that vague = bad. That if you can't measure it precisely, it doesn't count.

But the most important things in life — love, meaning, aliveness, growth — aren't precisely measurable. And yet they're everything.

The Lesson

Give yourself permission to pursue a direction without demanding a perfect destination. Ask: "What direction feels right?" Then start walking. The path will reveal itself.

Distinction #3: Correction vs. Self-Judgment

This might be the most important distinction of all — because it determines what happens when (not if) you fall short.

And you will fall short. We all do. The question is: What do you do next?

For most of my life, falling short triggered a cascade of self-judgment. Missing a goal wasn't just data — it was a verdict. Proof that I was undisciplined. Unfocused. Fundamentally flawed.

And here's the cruel irony: Self-judgment doesn't motivate change. It prevents it.

When you beat yourself up for missing a goal, you associate that goal with pain. Your nervous system learns: "This pursuit = suffering." And what does the nervous system do with things that cause suffering? It avoids them.

So the very act of judging yourself for falling short makes you less likely to try again.

The Distinction

Correction
Adjust & continue
vs
Self-Judgment
"I'm not good enough"

"Mastery is how quickly you correct — not how perfectly you perform."

The alternative is correction: treating every fall as information rather than indictment.

You missed a workout? That's not proof that you're lazy. It's data. What got in the way? What could you adjust? How might you make it easier next time?

You broke your diet? That's not proof that you lack willpower. It's feedback. What was happening emotionally? What need were you trying to meet? How can you meet that need differently?

Correction is clinical. Curious. Kind. It says: "Interesting. Let me adjust and continue."

Self-judgment is emotional. Harsh. Final. It says: "See? I knew I couldn't do this."

The Mastery Mindset

I once heard someone describe mastery as "the rate of correction, not the absence of error." That reframed everything for me.

Masters aren't people who never fail. They're people who fail, notice, adjust, and continue — faster than everyone else. Their secret isn't perfection. It's rapid, shame-free correction.

This distinction is especially vital at the start of a new year, when the temptation toward all-or-nothing thinking is strongest.

You're going to slip. You're going to have weeks where everything falls apart. The question isn't whether that will happen — it's how you respond when it does.

Will you spiral into judgment and give up? Or will you shrug, correct, and keep going?

The trajectory of your year — and your life — depends on that choice.

The Lesson

When you fall short, ask: "What can I learn and adjust?" Never: "What's wrong with me?" Correction creates momentum. Judgment creates quicksand.

• • •

What These Three Distinctions Add Up To

Here's what I've come to believe:

Most people don't fail at goals because they lack motivation, discipline, or intelligence. They fail because they're operating with the wrong mental models.

They aim for peaks when they should build baselines.
They demand perfection when they should follow direction.
They judge themselves when they should simply correct.

Shift these three distinctions, and everything changes.

You stop chasing unsustainable highs and start building unshakeable foundations. You stop waiting for perfect clarity and start moving with directional confidence. You stop treating setbacks as verdicts and start treating them as data.

That's the path to an extraordinary life. Not the dramatic, Instagram-worthy breakthroughs — but the quiet, compounding power of showing up, adjusting, and continuing.

As we step into 2025, I hope you'll carry these distinctions with you. Not as another set of rules to follow, but as a new way of relating to your own growth.

You're not behind. You're not broken. You're just learning to play a game that nobody taught you the real rules to.

Now you know them.

Here's to your extraordinary year ahead.

— Vishen
